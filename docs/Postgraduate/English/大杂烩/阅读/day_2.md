## Text 1

A proposal now before a **congressional** committee would create a national civic-service program for young adults who are neither studying nor securely employed. Its supporters describe the plan as a **strategic** response to a problem that is often discussed emotionally but rarely addressed with **precision**. The program is **intentionally** broad: participants could work in elder care, environmental repair, public libraries or neighborhood construction, while receiving **continuous** training and a modest wage. For those leaving the **juvenile** justice system, it could also provide an **avenue** back into ordinary community life.

The case for the program is **plausible**. Long periods without work can allow uncertainty to **drift** into discouragement. A young person who remains **idle** for months may lose not only income but also daily structure, professional contacts and confidence. A paid placement could **boost** employability, **foster** useful habits and help participants escape the **rut** of repeated rejection. Communities, meanwhile, often have a **surplus** of necessary tasks that local authorities lack the staff to perform.

Yet good intentions do not automatically produce good institutions. If the government decides to **proceed**, it must avoid turning participants into a cheap and **disposable** labor force. A program advertised as public service could quietly **oblige** young people to accept unpleasant work under threat of losing benefits. What looks like an **enviable** opportunity from a distance may feel like **humiliation** to someone who is ordered to clean public spaces while permanent employees receive better pay and protection.

There is also a more **subtle** danger. Because civic-service schemes generate favorable **publicity**, politicians may value visible activity more than lasting improvement. They may celebrate the number of placements while ignoring whether the jobs are merely **temporary**, whether participants gain recognized skills, or whether employers replace regular workers with subsidized recruits. Such doubts should not produce blanket **suspicion**, but they do justify careful design.

The best version of the policy would neither romanticize service nor dismiss it. It would set clear labor standards, provide independent complaints procedures and measure outcomes over several years. Excessive bureaucracy could **hinder** local experimentation, but weak oversight would invite exploitation. The real test is not whether the program keeps young people busy. It is whether it gives them greater control over what happens next.

## Text 2

When plans were announced to restore a large **predator** to a region where it had disappeared, the public debate quickly became more dramatic than the science. The animal’s population on that part of the **continent** had once fallen to a single **digit**, bringing it close to local **extinction**. Ecologists argued that its return could help control grazing animals and restore biological **diversity**. Opponents warned that farms, tourism and village life would be placed at risk.

Neither side was speaking only about biology. The animal carried a cultural **legacy** formed by folktales, schoolbooks and political speeches. It was **literally** presented in some stories as a creature that waits outside the human world, ready to cross the boundary whenever social order weakens. Its reputation for **cunning** made it **particularly** useful as a symbol. Online, each side formed a competing digital **tribe**, treating the other’s evidence as morally **inferior**. A damaged fence, a missing sheep or even a **weird** sound recorded at night could become proof of an entire worldview.

A university research team, supported by its **chancellor**, used **sophisticated** media analysis to **demonstrate** a recurring **pattern**. People with a strong **inclination** to fear the reintroduction tended to remember reports of attacks while forgetting reports of peaceful coexistence. Those engaged in **compulsive** news consumption were especially likely to overestimate rare events. This did not mean their concerns were false. It meant that attention itself was unevenly distributed.

The political problem is therefore not solved by simply asking which side is correct. A **liberal** society must permit citizens to object to policies that may alter their livelihoods. At the same time, no group should be allowed to **violate** environmental law or **obscure** evidence through intimidation. Some campaigners **condemn** every farmer as hostile to nature; some opponents treat every conservationist as an urban outsider. Both reactions make it harder to **integrate** ecological goals with local knowledge.

The government’s first plan was deliberately **tentative**. It offered compensation for livestock losses, funds for stronger fencing and a share of tourism **revenue** for affected communities. Critics called the proposal weak, while supporters feared that compromise would **stain** the moral case for restoration. Yet conflict is not necessarily evidence of failure. A policy that never **upset** anyone might simply be avoiding difficult choices. The challenge is to replace symbolic warfare with institutions capable of revising decisions as evidence changes.
